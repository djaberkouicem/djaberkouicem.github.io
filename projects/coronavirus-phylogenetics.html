Human Coronaviruses · Spike Protein · Evolutionary Relationships
Tools
NCBI · Clustal Omega · R (seqinr, ape)
Research Question How are the major human-infecting coronaviruses related to one another based on their Spike protein sequences, and does a Spike-based tree recover the established genus-level classification of these viruses (Alphacoronavirus vs. Betacoronavirus)?

Why Coronaviruses?

Seven coronaviruses are known to infect humans, ranging from mild seasonal colds (HCoV-229E, HCoV-NL63, HCoV-OC43) to severe epidemic and pandemic disease (SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2). Comparing their genetic relationships helps explain why some outbreaks were far more severe than others and how these viruses are grouped taxonomically.

Why the Spike Protein?

The Spike (S) protein mediates viral entry into host cells and is the main target of neutralizing antibodies and vaccines, which makes it a biologically meaningful and widely studied region for comparison. It is worth noting, however, that Spike evolves relatively quickly and is prone to recombination — a point revisited in the Limitations section below.

Methods

The workflow followed these steps:

  1. Spike protein sequences retrieved from NCBI Protein for the six viruses above.
  2. Multiple sequence alignment performed using Clustal Omega (EMBL-EBI).
  3. The aligned sequences were imported into R using the seqinr package.
  4. Pairwise genetic distances were calculated from the alignment using an identity-based distance matrix.
  5. A phylogenetic tree was constructed from the distance matrix using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) algorithm, implemented in the ape package.
  6. The resulting tree was compared against the established ICTV genus classification of these viruses.

(Raw sequence files and the full R script are kept private; this page summarizes methodology and results rather than publishing the complete implementation.)

Results

  • SARS-CoV-2 and SARS-CoV showed the smallest pairwise genetic distance among all six viruses (identity-based distance ≈ 0.48), consistent with their known close relationship as members of the Sarbecovirus subgenus.
  • HCoV-NL63 and HCoV-229E — the two Alphacoronaviruses in the set — also grouped together with a comparatively low distance (≈ 0.60), matching their shared genus.
  • HCoV-OC43, despite being a Betacoronavirus (Embecovirus subgenus), clustered with the Alphacoronavirus pair (NL63/229E) rather than with MERS-CoV or the SARS pair in this tree.
  • MERS-CoV formed the most distinct, longest branch in the tree relative to the other viruses tested.

Interpretation

Observed

The Spike-based tree correctly grouped SARS-CoV-2 with SARS-CoV, and separately grouped the two Alphacoronaviruses (NL63, 229E) together — both consistent with established taxonomy.

Interpretation / Hypothesis

The placement of HCoV-OC43 with the Alphacoronavirus pair, rather than with the other Betacoronaviruses, is inconsistent with ICTV genus classification. This is most plausibly explained by the Spike protein's fast evolutionary rate and known susceptibility to recombination among coronaviruses, combined with the simplifying assumptions of an identity-based distance and Neighbor-Joining approach — rather than by a genuine close relationship between OC43 and the Alphacoronaviruses.

Not directly established by this project

This project does not determine the true genome-wide evolutionary relationships between these viruses, nor does it use statistical support measures (e.g., bootstrap values) or a more conserved marker gene (e.g., RdRp), both of which are standard in rigorous coronavirus phylogenetics.

Limitations

  • The tree is based on a single protein (Spike) rather than whole-genome data or a more conserved gene such as RdRp, which is generally preferred for deep coronavirus phylogenetics.
  • The Neighbor-Joining method with an identity-based distance matrix is a simplifying approach; it does not correct for multiple substitutions at the same site and does not include bootstrap support values.
  • Spike is known to evolve quickly and to undergo recombination between coronavirus lineages, which can distort tree topology relative to the true evolutionary history — plausibly explaining the unexpected placement of HCoV-OC43.
  • Only six viruses were included; a broader sample of coronavirus sequences would give a more robust picture.

Conclusion

This project demonstrates a complete molecular phylogenetics workflow — from sequence retrieval, through multiple sequence alignment, to distance-based tree construction — applied to six clinically important human coronaviruses. The analysis correctly recovered the close relationship between SARS-CoV-2 and SARS-CoV, and between the two Alphacoronaviruses, but also revealed a limitation of Spike-based, distance-only phylogenetics: HCoV-OC43 did not group with its true genus. This discrepancy is itself an informative result, illustrating why more rigorous studies rely on conserved genes and statistically supported tree-building methods for deep viral phylogenetics.
